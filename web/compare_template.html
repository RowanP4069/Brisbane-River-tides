Measured vs predicted water height: how well did the predictions match the river?

The predictions page works out the tide along the river from the Brisbane Bar tide table. This page checks it: for each gauge, the water level that was measured against what the model predicted for the same place and time. Pick any period we have readings for. Hover over (or tap) a column heading for what it means; how this works is at the bottom.

This compares water height, not flow direction. The flow keeps running for a while after the level turns; for what paddlers have seen of the flow, see Report the flow (results).

How this works, and its limits
  • Predicted is the same model as the predictions page, worked out at each gauge's place on the river. Measured is what the gauges recorded: Brisbane City Council's river gauges (raw, unchecked) and Maritime Safety Queensland's Brisbane Bar gauge, saved daily since early November 2026.
  • Datum. The council gauges read metres AHD; the model gives metres above each place's own LAT. The gap between the two differs along the river, so it is measured: the average of measured minus predicted over all the readings. It's shown as "LAT below AHD" and stays the same whichever period you pick. Plots show the prediction shifted by it. The Bar reads LAT itself, so nothing is fitted there, and its average difference is the weather (wind, air pressure, surge) over the period.
  • Residual is measured minus predicted (minus that offset). Weather, surge and freshwater flow are all in the measurements and none are in the model, so this is an upper limit on the model's own error. Compare a gauge with the Bar to see how much is weather.
  • Timing of high and low water. The council gauges only send a reading when the level has moved a step (1, 2 or 5 cm; see the "step" column), so right at high or low water, where the level hardly moves, there are often no readings for an hour. Instead, the predicted curve within two hours of each turn is slid in time to fit the readings either side. Tested on made-up data: 5 cm steps make the river look about 3 minutes early (1–2 cm steps, under 1), and a real difference between the high-water and low-water lags shows up only about half as large. A few tides are too few to go on; the counts are in brackets.
  • Gauge floors. The Jindalee and Moggill gauges go silent for hours around low water and come back at the same level, while the tide elsewhere keeps falling: most likely the sensor sits above low water and dries out. Readings near that level (the dashed line), and the low waters it hides, are left out.
  • Range compares each rise or fall: measured height change divided by predicted. Over a spring–neap cycle (about two weeks) it shows whether the model's single ratio per place fits big and small tides alike.
